Keeping Pace

One operating model for a security department in an accelerating world, and why it has to be designed as a whole.

October 2026 · Draws together the earlier papers in this series

In brief

Business change and attacker activity are both speeding up. A security department organised as separate teams cannot keep up by working harder. It needs one team, working from one shared picture, acting within limits the business has set.

That model cannot be bought or decreed. It comes about only when service design, enterprise architecture and organisation design work together, because each shapes the others. Galbraith's Star Model holds them together. Underneath sits data: not one giant store, but a common view with shared definitions and a short list of critical questions that decides what reaches a decision-maker.

The starting point is the one every earlier paper shares. The business owns the risk. Security helps it stay within its risk appetite, the level of risk it has chosen to accept. Military ideas appear only where they add something, and each is explained where it first appears.

Part 1The pace problem

The department is squeezed from two sides. The business wants to change faster, and attackers are moving faster. Both land on the same people.

Two accelerations, one squeeze

The business releases digital services constantly, runs on cloud platforms and hundreds of suppliers, and is building AI into its products and work. Security exists so it can make those changes safely, so speed is part of the service. Meanwhile attackers are better organised and increasingly automated, and regulators expect serious incidents to be handled and reported within hours.

Attacks now move in days and minutes

Google's Mandiant team tracks how long attackers take to use a newly known weakness. The average fell from 63 days in 2018–19 to 5 days in 2023. Once inside, intruders move fast: CrowdStrike's 2025 report puts the average time to spread beyond the first machine at 48 minutes.

These figures come from companies that sell defences, but the direction is not in doubt. A process that needs a ticket, a weekly meeting and a sign-off is now slower than the attack it is meant to stop.

Regulators have set the clock

The EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and the UK's operational resilience rules expect firms to know their most important services, set limits on disruption, and report serious incidents fast. The hard part is not the form. It is knowing within hours that something is serious, and getting that fact to the person who must decide.

The gap

The pace of attack and business change rises steeply. The speed at which separate teams can notice, decide and act rises slowly, because every signal travels through the organisation chart. The space between is risk nobody chose.

We need to do something, and the something is design.

Part 2Where today's operating models strain

Most departments are organised as separate teams with their own tools, data and managers. That shape slows the response to issues, and it makes the department hard to lead and hard to work in.

Responding to issues

Information climbs and crosses. Identity, network, cloud, monitoring and policy teams work as "silos": information travels up and down each one but rarely across. Attackers ignore those boundaries. So a warning seen by one team must climb one chain of command and come down another before anyone acts.

Services are framed as controls. Names like "vulnerability management" describe the mechanism, not what the business owner wants, which is to know where they are exposed in time to act. Framed as controls, services get measured by whether controls exist, and security starts behaving as if it owns the risk.

Risk decisions are made in the wrong place. A delayed patch or a policy exception is a risk decision, often taken quietly inside a team by someone who does not own the risk. Where security and technology share one leader, the business owner may never see it.

Policy does strategy's job. Strategy chooses what matters most and what will not be done; policy sets what must always or never happen. When a compliance list stands in for strategy, everything is equally important, so the department cannot go faster anywhere.

Everything escalates. When nobody has written down who may decide what, the safe choice is to escalate. Leaders become a bottleneck, buried in issues they need not see.

Managing people

One person, three hats. The head of department is CISO (sets security policy and checks it is followed), service provider (runs services the business uses to meet that policy) and head of department (decides where money and people go). The first is an oversight role; the other two are doing and owning roles.

Measures count activity. Alerts closed and patches applied answer "are we busy?", not "is the business safe?". Goodhart's law applies: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

People fear reporting. Where risk and audit are seen as hunting for fault, problems stay hidden, and a department that cannot surface problems cannot learn.

Authority is unclear. Without written limits, people either take risks they should not or escalate everything. Both are exhausting.

Teams are reshuffled. Frequent reorganisations move people between boxes. People resist losing what made them effective more than they resist change. Stable teams that take on new work cope better.

All work is managed the same way. Repeatable work like granting access suits a production line. Expert work like incident response does not. Managing both alike frustrates everyone.

One root cause: designed in pieces

Each strain has a local fix, and departments try them one at a time. They rarely stick, because the pieces were designed separately and pull against each other. Galbraith's Star Model, explained in Part 3, makes this visible.

Part 3A common operating model, and what it takes to build one

The answer is a common operating model: one way of working shared by the department and the parts of the business it serves. It only comes about when service design, enterprise architecture and organisation design are done together.

Three disciplines, one design

An operating model describes how an organisation is set up to deliver its strategy: which capabilities matter most, what is done centrally, which forums decide what. Every such choice is also a technology choice and a service choice, so all three disciplines should write it together. A simple test: an architect, an organisation designer and a service designer should each be able to derive their own design from it. If not, it is a strategy statement.

  • Service design starts with the user, here the business owner, and what they are trying to do.
  • Enterprise architecture starts with what the organisation must be able to do, and the data and technology behind it.
  • Organisation design starts with how people are grouped, governed and motivated.

Each discipline's blind spot is the other two. A service blueprint, which draws a service from what the user sees down to the systems underneath, shows where they meet.

A good service is desirable, feasible and viable. Service design speaks for the first, architecture the second, organisation design the third. Service design sets the test; the other two set the terms.

Conway's law

In 1968 Melvin Conway observed that organisations build systems that mirror their own communication structures. So if you design the architecture without the organisation, you have still designed both, one by accident. Used deliberately, it works the other way: shape the teams you want and the architecture follows.

Galbraith's Star: the frame that holds it together

Jay Galbraith's Star Model says an organisation has five design points that must line up: strategy (direction and what will not be done), structure (grouping and who decides), processes (how work and information flow across teams), rewards (what is measured and recognised) and people (skills and development). Strategy comes first. Change one point alone and the others pull it back: a new structure with old rewards produces old behaviour in new boxes.

The Star treats information and technology lightly, yet Galbraith also described organisations as information processing systems. So information is the Star's implied sixth point, at the centre. That is where architecture is strongest, while rewards and people, which architecture frameworks barely mention, are where organisation design is strongest.

The symbiosis is clearest when one decision is followed around the Star. Take the open question from earlier papers: organise by capability (identity, network, cloud) or by mission (protect payments)?

The likely answer is both: keep capability teams as the home for skills and tools, and form mission teams across them for the services that matter most.

The shape of the model: front, middle and back

Services face the business at the front. A security "factory" of capabilities sits in the middle, with separate expert cells for investigative work. Engineering, data and platforms sit at the back. Business operations runs across all three and owns the operating model, measured on keeping the layers in balance.

The business owns the risk

The firm's enterprise risk management framework is not one of the layers. It is a rulebook running through all of them, starting from the board's risk appetite.

So services should be named for what the business owner is trying to do: "find and fix my weaknesses", not "vulnerability management". Each should state what security does and what the business must still do, and be judged by whether the business stays within appetite.

Direction and freedom

The model only goes faster if people can act without waiting for permission. That needs a direction everyone can carry in their head and written limits on what each team may decide. This is where military experience adds most.

Military ideas · Intent, mission command and arcs of fire

Commander's intent is a short statement of what a mission must achieve and why, so people can act sensibly when the plan breaks. Each level writes its intent inside the one above, and people read intent two levels up. Mission command is the British Army's way of leading: say what and why, leave the how to those closest to the problem. An arc of fire is the sector a soldier may act in without asking, set in advance.

In a bank, the business's risk appetite is the natural source of the arcs, and the business owner draws them in calm conditions. As David Marquet warns in Turn the Ship Around!, authority can only be pushed down as fast as competence and clarity go with it, so arcs must be rehearsed.

Two loops, one picture

With direction and freedom in place, the department can run continuously. A slow loop, Continuous Threat Exposure Management (CTEM), keeps finding and fixing weaknesses that matter to the business. A fast loop stops attacks in progress.

Military idea · The OODA loop

John Boyd's model of decision-making: observe, orient, decide, act. Boyd's key point was orientation. When people share the same understanding and intent, they can act without asking, so speed follows from shared understanding.

The three hats also find their place. As CISO, the head of security helps the business set its intent and checks both loops stay within policy, with the risk function. As service provider, they run the picture and loops, reviewed with business owners. As head of department, they design the Star so the model can run. Business owners should always know which hat they are talking to.

Part 4Data: a common view, not a common store

Data sits at the centre of the Star. The answer is not to pour it into one place, but to give everyone a common view: shared definitions, open access and a short list of questions that decides what matters.

Three ways to hold the data

The signal in Figure 6 travelled slowly on the left because each team held its data in its own terms. The fix is shared meaning, not necessarily shared storage.

Military examples · Delta and JDN 1/23

Delta is Ukraine's live shared battlefield map, fed by units, drones, satellites and allies. Ukrainian officials say it cut the time from spotting a target to acting from up to 72 hours to about two minutes. It worked because everyone who contributes also benefits, and machines take the first look. The UK's 2023 guidance on intelligence gathering, JDN 1/23, says the effort should not be "artificially divided" by domain, and that making sense of information is "not subordinate to collection". The data does not belong to whoever collected it.

The data spine

Sharing everything creates a new problem: if everyone sees everything, nobody sees anything. The answer is a "data spine", a thin rigid structure everything else attaches to. It has three parts: a short list of critical questions, each with an owner and a decision; shared definitions (one identity per person and system, one list of critical services, one severity scale); and sorting rules for who must hear what, how fast.

Critical questions

Military idea · CCIRs

Commander's Critical Information Requirements are questions a leader says in advance they must have answered, because the answer changes a decision. They are "the things you wake the commander up for". Some ask about the threat, some about our own defences, and a related list says what the other side must never learn.

Good CCIRs are tied to a decision, answerable yes or no, specific, dated, and few. In a bank there are two kinds of "commander": business risk owners and the head of security. For payments, they might include:

TypeCritical questionDecision it serves
ThreatIs there evidence an attacker has access to the live payments environment?Switch to the backup payments route
Own defencesHas monitoring covered less than the agreed share of payments systems for more than a day?Fund extra monitoring, or pause a change
Own defencesHas this incident met the threshold for a major incident?Notify the regulator within DORA's hours
GuardDetails of monitoring gaps and live investigationsNever shared outside need-to-know

CCIRs complement key risk indicators: a KRI shows the trend, a CCIR says a decision is due. Triage then sorts signals by urgency in three sifts.

Traceability

Every security service should trace to a risk the business owns, through the control it supports and where that control lives. This chain lets a deferred fix reach the real risk owner, and shows where the CISO is assuring their own services.

Measures: the mission, not the activity

Measures should show whether the mission is being achieved. The middle layer is new: it checks whether critical information reaches the right person in time, tracking delays, misses, false alarms and off-list escalations. Pair measures across each tension, such as exception requests alongside work-arounds found, so one side cannot hide its cost in another.

Information load, in Galbraith's terms

Galbraith's 1974 work explains why this hangs together. When uncertainty rises, an organisation must either reduce the information that flows up or increase its capacity to handle it. The data spine does both.

One caution: a picture showing every weakness is also what an attacker most wants. It needs the strongest protection the firm has.

Part 5Bringing it together

Back on the Star, the operating model is one design: business intent above, its limits below, data at the centre and a specific job at each point.

The business owns the risk. Security gives it the picture, the questions and the speed to stay within its appetite as the world accelerates.

Where to start

No big-bang reorganisation is needed. Start with one critical service, such as payments:

  1. Write the intent chain with the business owner.
  2. Agree the arcs: what security may do alone, and what goes to the owner first.
  3. Agree a handful of critical questions, alongside existing KRIs.
  4. Fix the shared definitions those questions depend on.
  5. Form one mission team across the existing capability teams.
  6. Start measuring time from a question being answered to the owner knowing.
  7. Give each of the three hats its own forum.
  8. Rehearse in an exercise, refine, and move to the next service.

Where the military lessons stop

  • The commander sits outside security. Priorities come from the business. Security runs the loops but cannot set their purpose.
  • The aim is to stay within limits, not to win. Sometimes the right answer is to accept a risk.
  • Faster is not always better. Taking payments offline in two minutes could do more harm than the attacker.
  • There are many commanders. Several business owners, risk, the board and regulators each need their own short list.
  • Someone must check independently. Risk and audit must see the questions and thresholds, so the filter never hides bad news.

Have a taste: open questions

  • Which actions may security take alone, and who can move that line during an incident?
  • Who owns the common picture: security, technology or the risk function?
  • Who owns each set of critical questions, and how many can one owner carry? Do they belong in the risk framework or in security's procedures?
  • How much triage can safely be automated?
  • If a security service is late or poor, who carries the breach of appetite?
  • Under a combined technology and security leader, how does the CISO role keep an independent line?
