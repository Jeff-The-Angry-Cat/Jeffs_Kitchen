Signal, Not Noise

CCIRs and the data spine of a one-team security department · October 2026

Introduction

A shared picture only helps if the right things flow through it. This paper argues that a short, agreed list of critical questions, which the armed forces call CCIRs, is what turns a flood of security data into a working “data spine” for the whole department.

The previous paper, Sensing and Responding: Security as One Network, argued that a security department should work as one team around one shared picture of what is happening. It borrowed from Ukraine’s Delta system, where everyone adds what they see and everyone can see the result.

That raises an obvious problem. A modern bank produces billions of log lines, thousands of alerts and hundreds of reports a day. If everyone adds everything and everyone sees everything, nobody sees anything. The picture becomes noise. Security teams already know this feeling as “alert fatigue”.

The armed forces met the same problem long before computers. Their answer has three parts, and each maps onto something a department can design:

  • Direction. Everyone knows where the whole organisation is going and why. In the military this is the commander’s intent. In a department it is strategy, and Galbraith’s Star Model says strategy is where every other design choice starts.
  • Freedom to act. Teams are trusted to act within that direction without going back for approval. The military calls this mission command.
  • A filter. Leaders say in advance exactly which pieces of information they must have, because those pieces trigger their decisions. Everything else is handled locally or stored. This is what CCIRs do.

The three depend on each other. Intent without freedom creates a queue at the leader’s door. Freedom without a filter leaves leaders blind, or buried. A filter without intent has nothing to filter for.

The paper explains each military idea in plain terms, then shows how they translate to a security department in a financial services firm, how they shape the department’s metrics, and what they ask of the organisation’s design. It keeps the starting point of the earlier papers: the business owns the risk, and security works within the business’s risk appetite.

Direction: strategy and commander’s intent

Everything else in this paper hangs off one thing: a clear, shared statement of where the department is going and why. Without it, there is no way to tell which information is critical and which is noise.

Where Galbraith comes in. Jay Galbraith’s Star Model, used in Designing the Whole, has five points: strategy, structure, processes, rewards and people. Strategy sits at the top for a reason. Galbraith treats it as the source of the criteria for every other choice: how to group teams, how work and information should flow, what to reward, and what skills to hire. A department that skips this step ends up designing each point on its own, and the points pull against each other.

What the military adds: commander’s intent. Strategy documents are often long and written for boards. The armed forces needed something every soldier could carry in their head. They call it commander’s intent. The British Army’s field manual on command defines it, “at its simplest”, as “a statement of the outcome that the Commander wishes to accomplish” (Wavell Room, citing Army Field Manual: Command, 2017). A good intent statement usually covers three things:

  1. Purpose: why we are doing this.
  2. Key tasks: the few things that must happen for it to work.
  3. End state: what “done” looks like.

The point is that when the plan breaks, which it always does, people can still act sensibly because they know what the plan was for.

Intent is nested. Each level writes its intent inside the intent of the level above, and officers are taught to understand the intent “two levels up”. A platoon commander knows not only what their company commander wants, but what the battalion is trying to achieve.

What this means in a bank. The security department’s intent does not start with security. It sits inside the business’s strategy and risk appetite. A plausible chain looks like this:

LevelWho writes itExample of intent
FirmBoard and executiveGrow digital payments while staying within agreed risk appetite and impact tolerances
Business serviceThe business risk owner for paymentsCustomers can always make payments; no outage longer than the agreed tolerance; no material fraud loss
Security departmentThe head of security, in all three of their rolesKeep the firm’s most important services within appetite by seeing threats early and acting before harm is done
Security teamTeam leadsFor example: no attacker keeps a foothold in the payments environment for more than an agreed time

The example wording is illustrative, not a recommendation. What matters is the shape: each line can be traced back to the one above it, and the business owner’s line is where security’s purpose comes from.

Mission command: freedom to act within the intent

Once people know the intent, they must be trusted to act on it without asking first. That is mission command, and it only works if its limits are written down in advance.

What it is. Mission command is the British Army’s way of leading. Leaders say what must be achieved and why, give people the resources to do it, and leave the how to those closest to the problem. The idea is usually traced to the Prussian army of the 1800s, where it was called Auftragstaktik (roughly, “mission tactics”). The army’s reason was practical: by the time a request for orders travelled up the chain and back, the situation had changed.

It is often misread as “leaders step back”. In practice it asks more of leaders, not less. They must:

  • state their intent clearly enough that people can act on it alone;
  • set the limits of freedom clearly, so people know where their authority ends;
  • trust people to use judgement, and back them when they do so in good faith;
  • stay informed, so they can step in when the situation moves beyond those limits.

Arcs of fire. A useful military image here is the “arc of fire”: the sector a soldier is allowed to fire into without asking. Inside the arc they act on their own judgement. Outside it they must ask. The arc is set in advance and everyone knows it.

What this means in a bank. The business’s risk appetite is the natural source of the department’s arcs of fire. The earlier paper suggested writing down which actions security may take on its own authority and which must go to the business owner first. For example:

Inside the arc: act, then tellOutside the arc: ask first
Lock a compromised staff accountTake a customer-facing payment service offline
Isolate an infected laptopBlock a whole supplier’s network access
Block a known malicious web addressTell a regulator, customers or the press
Force a password reset for a user groupAccept a known weakness past its fix date

The split is illustrative. The point is that the boundary is drawn by the business owner, in advance, in calm conditions, rather than negotiated during an incident.

The catch. Mission command creates a new problem. If teams act on their own, how does the leader know what is going on? And how does a team know when something has crossed the line and the leader must be told now? The armed forces’ answer to that question is the CCIR.

CCIRs: the questions that wake the commander

A CCIR is a question the leader has said, in advance, they must have answered because the answer will change a decision they have to make. Everything that answers a CCIR goes straight to them. Everything else does not.

What the letters mean. CCIR stands for Commander’s Critical Information Requirements. The phrase is clumsy but each word earns its place:

  • Commander’s: they belong to the person accountable for the decision, not to the analysts.
  • Critical: they are the few things that matter most, not everything that is interesting.
  • Information requirements: they are stated as needs (“I must know...”), not as data that happens to be available.

A staff officer will often describe them as “the things you wake the commander up for”.

The parts of a CCIR. Doctrine splits them by what they are about:

PartStands forWhat it asks aboutMilitary example
PIRPriority Intelligence RequirementThe other side: what are they doing, or about to do?Will the enemy tank division send a regiment south of the river in the next 24 hours?
FFIRFriendly Force Information RequirementOurselves: what state are our own people and kit in?Is 1st Battalion still fit to fight, and where is it, if the enemy breaks through?
EEFIEssential Elements of Friendly InformationWhat the other side must not learn about usThe planned river crossing point and time

The first two examples are adapted from a teaching article on CCIRs (Armchair General, Tactics 101: the power of CCIRs). US and NATO doctrine always treat PIRs and FFIRs as the two parts of a CCIR. Some versions list EEFIs as a third part; others treat them as a separate, protective list. For this paper the distinction does not matter: EEFIs are the mirror image, telling people what to guard rather than what to report.

What makes a good CCIR. The same article and the wider doctrine share five tests:

  1. It is tied to a decision. Each one supports a specific choice the leader expects to face, often called a “decision point”. If no decision depends on it, it is just an information requirement, not a critical one.
  2. It is a question. “Will the enemy cross the river?” can be answered yes or no. “Enemy activity” cannot.
  3. It is specific. It names a time, a place and a thing to watch.
  4. It has a shelf life. Doctrine uses the phrase “latest time information is of value”: after that point the answer no longer helps, and the people and sensors watching for it can be pointed elsewhere.
  5. There are few of them. The article warns against “laundry lists” of nice-to-know items. A short list is what makes it a filter.

Why it is a filter, not just a list. CCIRs do three jobs at once:

  • They point the sensors. Collection effort, the “task” step from the ISR doctrine in the previous paper, is aimed at answering them first.
  • They tell everyone what to push up. A junior soldier who sees the enemy regiment at the river knows, without asking, that this must go to the commander immediately, and that a broken-down truck elsewhere need not.
  • They protect mission command. Because everyone knows what the leader must hear, the leader does not need to hear everything else. Teams get on with their work, and the leader’s attention is kept for the decisions only they can make.

This is the triage a joined-up department needs. A CCIR does not just say what matters; it creates a sorting rule that anyone, or any machine, can apply.

CCIRs in a security department

In a bank, CCIRs come in two sets, because there are two kinds of commander. The business risk owner decides how much risk to run; the head of security decides how to run the department. Each needs their own short list.

Who the commanders are. The earlier papers established that the business owns the risk and security works within its appetite. So the most important CCIRs belong to business risk owners: “tell me when my service is at risk of moving outside appetite”. The head of security, wearing their head-of-department hat, has a second set about the department’s own ability to protect: people, tools and coverage. Wearing the CISO hat, they also need to know when the business itself is drifting from policy.

Translating the three parts.

  • PIRs become threat and exposure questions. What attackers are doing, or what weaknesses they could use, against the services that matter most. These draw on threat intelligence and the CTEM exposure loop from the previous paper.
  • FFIRs become questions about our own defences. Is monitoring working where it must? Are key controls in place? Do we have the people to respond tonight? These are the questions a siloed department is often worst at answering, because each team only knows its own slice.
  • EEFIs become what we must never let an attacker learn. For example, where our monitoring has gaps, the details of a live investigation, or how our most sensitive systems are set up. They tell people what not to put in a ticket, a chat channel or an email to a supplier.

Worked example: protecting payments. Suppose the payments business owner has an intent that customers can always make payments, with no outage longer than the agreed impact tolerance. Their decisions, and the CCIRs that support them, might look like this:

Decision the owner may faceTypeCCIR, written as a questionShelf life
Do we switch to the backup payments route?PIRIs there evidence an attacker has access to the live payments environment?Until the current threat assessment is reviewed
Do we accept a delay to fixing a known weakness?PIRIs anyone known to be exploiting this weakness against firms like us?Until the fix date
Do we fund extra monitoring for payments?FFIRHas monitoring covered less than the agreed share of payments systems for more than a day?Standing, reviewed each quarter
Do we pause a planned change to the payments system?FFIRWould the change leave payments without working backup or monitoring, even briefly?Until the change window
Must we notify the regulator?FFIRHas an incident met the firm’s threshold for a major incident?Standing
(Guard, do not report)EEFIDetails of monitoring gaps and of any live investigation in paymentsStanding

The wording and thresholds are placeholders for the business owner and security to agree. The value is in the pattern: every question names the decision it serves, and every one can be answered yes or no.

Some CCIRs are set from outside. Regulators effectively set some of them already. The EU’s Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) requires major ICT incidents to be reported to the regulator within tight deadlines, measured in hours. “Has this incident become a major incident?” is therefore a CCIR whether or not anyone writes it down. Writing it down makes sure the right people hear in time.

How CCIRs relate to risk indicators. Most banks already have key risk indicators (KRIs), which track risk levels over time against appetite thresholds. CCIRs are not a replacement. A KRI tells you which way the trend is going. A CCIR tells you that a decision is now due. In practice, a KRI crossing its appetite limit is one of the most natural CCIRs a business owner can have, so the two should be designed together, from the same risk appetite statement.

The data spine: CCIRs as filter and triage

Put together, intent, mission command and CCIRs give the department a “data spine”: a thin, agreed structure that carries intent down to every team and carries only critical information back up. It is what the common picture from the previous paper hangs on.

Reading the diagram: intent and appetite set the CCIRs, the CCIRs tell every sensor and team what to watch for first, and everything seen goes into the common picture. Every signal then passes one triage question, “does this answer a CCIR?” (the dashed line shows the CCIRs setting that rule). Yes means push it to the decision-maker now. Within a team’s own mission means act under mission command and record it. Everything else is recorded, not pushed. The decision-maker’s choices then refine the intent and CCIRs, closing the loop.

Why “spine” and not “platform”. A spine is not the whole body. It is the small, rigid part that everything else attaches to. In data terms it has three parts:

  1. The questions. The current CCIRs, each with its owner, its decision, its threshold and its review date. This list is short and changes on purpose.
  2. The shared definitions. To recognise a CCIR automatically, every tool must describe the world the same way: one identity for each person and system, one list of which services are critical, one way of rating severity. This is the “shared map grid” from the mesh in the previous paper. Without it, the same event looks different in every team’s tool and no rule can catch it.
  3. The sorting rules. The triage logic that turns a CCIR into something people and machines can apply: what counts as “met”, who must hear, how fast, and through which route.

Triage, step by step. Medical triage sorts patients by urgency so the most serious are seen first, not by who arrived first. The data spine does the same with signals:

  • First sift by machine. Most signals never need a person. Automated rules match them against the CCIR thresholds, just as Delta’s add-on scans video for vehicles. This is where most noise is removed.
  • Second sift by the team that owns the mission. Signals that might matter go to the team whose mission they touch. That team decides, within its arc of fire, whether to act, record or escalate.
  • Escalation only on a CCIR. A leader is interrupted only when a CCIR is met, or when a team judges something is so unusual that the CCIRs may be wrong. That second case is itself useful: it tells the leader the list needs updating.

Nothing is thrown away. The previous paper noted that JDN 1/23 asks for all collected data to be treated as potentially valuable. The spine does not delete what it does not push. It keeps everything in the common picture, where analysts can look for slow patterns and where risk and audit can check the filter is honest. The filter decides what interrupts people, not what is kept.

Information load, in Galbraith’s terms. Galbraith’s early work treated organisations as information processing systems (Organization Design: An Information Processing View, 1974). When uncertainty rises, an organisation either reduces how much information must flow to the top, or increases its capacity to process it. His options include self-contained tasks, vertical information systems and lateral networks. The spine uses all three: mission command makes teams more self-contained, CCIRs make the vertical flow narrow and sharp, and the common picture is the lateral network.

Metrics: measuring the mission, not the activity

If CCIRs are the questions, metrics should tell the leader whether the mission is being achieved. Most security dashboards do something else: they count activity, such as alerts closed, patches applied and training completed.

Activity counts are not useless, but they answer “are we busy?” rather than “are we winning?”. They also invite the trap known as Goodhart’s law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. A team rewarded for closing alerts quickly will close them quickly, whether or not the threat has gone.

Three layers of measure. A department built around intent and CCIRs needs measures at three levels, each serving a different reader:

LayerQuestion it answersExamplesMain reader
MissionAre the services we protect staying within appetite?Time each critical service spent outside risk appetite or impact tolerance; losses from security incidents against appetiteBusiness risk owners, board
SpineIs critical information reaching the right person in time to decide?Time from a CCIR being met to the decision-maker knowing; CCIRs missed and found later; false alarms per CCIRHead of security, business owners
ActivityIs each team doing its work well?Alerts handled, weaknesses fixed by their due date, monitoring coverageTeam leads

The top layer is the one that matters to the business. The bottom layer is how teams manage themselves under mission command; it should mostly stay with them. The middle layer is new, and it is the one this paper argues for.

Measuring the spine. The middle layer asks whether the CCIR system itself works. Five measures are worth tracking:

  1. Time to the decision-maker. How long from the moment a CCIR is met to the moment the right person knows. This is the security version of Delta’s “spot to act” time from the previous paper.
  2. Misses. CCIRs that were met but only discovered afterwards, for example in a post-incident review.
  3. False alarms. Times a CCIR was raised but turned out not to be met. Some are healthy; a stream of them teaches people to ignore the alarm.
  4. Escalations outside the CCIRs. Issues pushed to leaders that did not meet any CCIR. Many of these mean either the CCIRs are wrong or people do not trust their own authority.
  5. CCIRs that never fire. A question that has not been met in a year may be well controlled, or may be the wrong question. Either way it deserves a review.

Measuring mission command. The same data shows whether teams are using their freedom. If almost every action goes up for approval, the arcs of fire are too narrow or not trusted. If teams regularly act outside them, the arcs are unclear. Both are design problems, not discipline problems.

Measures follow the intent. When the intent changes, for example a new strategy or a new service becomes critical, the CCIRs and the measures should change with it. A dashboard that has not changed in two years is a sign the department is measuring what it can count, not what it needs to know.

Back to the Star Model: what has to change

The data spine is not a tool to buy. It only works if all five points of Galbraith’s Star are designed to support it, and if they all start from the same strategy and intent.

Designing the Whole argued that information and technology are the Star’s implied sixth point, the thing that holds the other five together. The data spine is a concrete version of that point for a security department. Each of the five points then has a specific job:

  • Strategy comes first. A clear, nested intent is what decides which questions are critical. If the strategy is vague, the CCIRs will be either too many or too general, and the filter fails.
  • Structure is where mission command lives. Galbraith’s structure includes the distribution of power: who may decide what. Grouping teams around missions, such as protecting payments or protecting customer data, gives each team a natural set of CCIRs to watch and a clear arc of fire. This echoes the previous paper’s open question about organising by mission rather than by tool.
  • Processes carry the spine day to day: the triage rules, the escalation routes, and a regular review where business owners and security agree which CCIRs to add, change or retire.
  • Rewards shape whether people trust the spine. If people are praised for escalating everything, or blamed when an honest alarm proves false, they will flood leaders with noise or stay quiet. Reward contributing to the picture and making sound local decisions.
  • People need the judgement to act within intent. That comes from training and practice: exercises where teams rehearse what is inside their arc, what counts as a CCIR, and what to do when the situation fits neither.

The three roles of the head of security from the earlier paper map onto this neatly. As CISO, they help the business write its CCIRs and assure the business is within policy. As service provider, they run the spine and the triage for the business. As head of department, they design the five points of the Star so the department can run it.

Where the military comparison stops

CCIRs translate well, but four differences need care.

  • There is more than one commander. A military unit has one commander who sets the CCIRs. A bank has many business risk owners, a chief risk officer, a board and regulators, each with their own decisions. The department will need a small number of CCIR sets, one per critical service or risk owner, and a way to settle conflicts between them.
  • Everything wants to be critical. In a firm where nobody wants to be blamed for not reporting something, CCIR lists tend to grow until they stop filtering. Someone must own the list and be willing to cut it. Doctrine’s “latest time information is of value” helps: every CCIR should carry a date when it will be reviewed or retired.
  • Not reporting must be safe. A soldier who uses their judgement within intent is protected by the culture of mission command. In a regulated firm, people may fear that handling something locally will look like hiding it. The rule should be plain: everything is recorded in the shared picture; only CCIRs are pushed to leaders. Nothing is hidden, but not everything interrupts.
  • Someone still checks independently. As in the earlier papers, the department both runs the CCIR system and, as CISO, assures that the business is within policy. Risk and audit functions should be able to see the CCIRs, the thresholds and the record of how they were met, so that the filter cannot quietly become a way to keep bad news from the top.

Have a taste: open questions

  • Who owns each set of CCIRs: the business risk owner, the head of security, or both together? And who can change them during an incident?
  • How many CCIRs can one business owner realistically carry? The military rule of thumb is “a handful”; is that right for a bank?
  • Should CCIRs live in the enterprise risk management framework, alongside risk appetite and KRIs, or in the security department’s own operating procedures?
  • How much of the triage can be automated safely, and which CCIRs must always be judged by a person?
  • How should EEFIs sit alongside the firm’s existing information classification scheme?
